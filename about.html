A Critical Manifesto: Delight, Instruct, and What Else?

Plato refutes all arts as an imitation of nature, the ultimate creative force that has spawned into existence what we perceive as reality. Aristotle praises our inclination to imitate and asserts that our work should strive to produce higher fidelity interpretations of what nature has presented us as “life.” Mimetics takes the foreground of what makes art essential to, and effective in producing understanding within humankind. This line of thought follows through into Philip Sidney’s presentation of the arts as a tool to bring forth delight and instruction. Oscar Wilde, on the cusp of postmodernism, challenges the artist to force nature into obsolescence with their own sheer creative will. I’m not sure that this is possible. Through the amalgamation of this variety of perspectives, I have come to believe that art is in itself a sensation, an effect of a series of motions and not a motion in itself.

It is my opinion that the physical and material nature of art has clouded our understanding of its essence. Literary critics such as Horace and Alexander Pope emphasize the importance of undertaking a rigorous process in the pursuit of producing great artwork. They acknowledge that art is the product of great effort, but this diminishes the arts to be seen as the endpoint of a sensation, opposed to the physical translation of a series of sensations experienced by and crafted from the artist’s own experience. Art is to be perceived, and through that process of perception can never be truly static. Art is the transference of ideas from artist to audience.

The cosmology of pre-colonial West African societies, such as the Yoruba people of Nigeria, emphasizes that those we call “artists” in our Eurocentric civilizations are only intermediaries, creating objects to house spirits that have their own desires and purposes. Art objects are alive through ritual, have their own agency, and act upon their audience independently of the original maker. For example, in the case of Frederick Douglass’ “What to a Slave is the Fourth of July” speech, the art itself and the process of its construction go well beyond the words of the speech itself. The “art” of this speech is not only defined by Douglass’ awareness of rhetorical strategy, the use of meter, or allusions to the Western canon of literature. The essence of the speech as a work of art lies within the life of Frederick Douglass, his journey into literacy, the political reality of Black and brown people in the Americas and worldwide, his voice, and the physical context of its delivery. A speech written with the same rhetorical fidelity but absent of that content could not be considered as impressive a feat of artistic creation.

In Aristotle’s Nicomachean Ethics, Aristotle sets out to teach his son a lesson on attaining happiness and walks readers through the attainment of what he calls “the good.” Aristotle outlines that happiness is achieved through the process of looking inward, determining your virtues, and pointing your soul in the direction of those virtues. He asserts that through this process the phenomenon that is happiness will arise. Aristotle emphasizes that happiness is not a possession to be had; it cannot be held and is not easily measured. It is a result emerging from the ongoing process of defining and acting in accord with self-determined virtues. Aristotle makes an analogy using a wheel that reminds me of Duchamp and his fellow Dadaist perceptions on art. Happiness is not a wheel itself; it is also not the spinning of the wheel. Happiness is not what makes the wheel spin either. Happiness is the effect of the wheel spinning, and it seems to me that art follows this same ethic.

Much like the spinning of two wheels on a bicycle creates balance facilitated by forward momentum, art emerges not from a single cause but from the dynamic interplay of creator, medium, and audience. It is this emergent quality of art that is easily overlooked through a critique of its aesthetics. The desire to freeze time and space around an art object robs it of its purpose as described by the classical philosophers. Unlike humans in their mortality, nature has no known beginning or end, and so our critiques of art and its purposes must acknowledge that reality. Art is not the product of an artist. It is the communicable sensation of an artist’s experience that emerges from the ongoing of their existence. And so that communicable sensation in itself is ongoing, like nature.
